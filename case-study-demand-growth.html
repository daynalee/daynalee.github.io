Advertiser Growth

Growing a Demand Book Past a Saturated Anchor Title

A casual puzzle advertiser in my book had one title carrying almost all their spend, and it was running out of room. I ran a weekly cadence against a tracked recommendation log, diversified spend into a second title, and moved them onto a longer measurement window. The quarter closed at their highest spend on record, with cost per install down and return on ad spend up.

Situation
One title carried nearly all of the advertiser's spend, and it had saturated. Effective CPM had tripled and there was nowhere left to scale.
Action
Weekly cadence against a tracked recommendation log, creative breakdowns to find what was actually fatiguing, spend diversified into a second title, and a move to a longer ROAS window.
Result
Record spend quarter, second title scaled 6x efficiently, CPI down and D7 ROAS up on the first cohort measured the new way.
Overview

This is how I actually grow a demand account. Not a single clever idea, but a cadence that compounds: meet on a schedule, bring recommendations that are written down, track whether they were implemented, and check that the basics are still in place before proposing anything new.

The advertiser here is a casual puzzle portfolio in my book. I have kept them unnamed and left out absolute spend figures, since those are their numbers rather than mine. The relative movements are real.

Context

Problem Statement

The account looked healthy on the surface. Spend was steady and the team was happy. Underneath, almost all of that spend sat on a single flagship title that had saturated its addressable audience. Effective CPM on it had tripled, which meant every additional dollar was buying progressively worse users. Steady spend on a saturating title is not stability, it is a cliff with a flat approach.

My Role

I own the relationship and the growth plan. I am not the one placing bids day to day. My job is to see the pattern before the advertiser feels it, bring a diagnosis they trust, and get the recommendation implemented, which is usually the harder half.

Why It Was Hard

  • The advertiser had no reason to believe anything was wrong, so the first conversation was about a problem they could not yet see
  • The second title was unproven and asking for budget to move there meant asking them to spend into uncertainty
  • Their measurement window was too short to show the value of the users the new title was bringing in
The Cadence

Everything below runs on a standing weekly call with the advertiser's UA team, plus a quarterly business review. The call is not a status update. It has the same four parts every week, which is what makes it useful:

1. What changed since last week

Delivery, spend pacing, cost per install, and return on ad spend against target, per title rather than blended. Blended numbers hide exactly the problem I was looking for here, because a saturating title and a scaling title average out to "fine."

2. The recommendation tracker

This is the piece that does the most work and gets the least credit. Every recommendation I make goes into a shared tracker with:

  • The recommendation itself, written specifically enough to be actioned without me in the room
  • Owner and target implementation date, so it does not sit in a meeting note
  • A best practices check, a simple confirmation that the underlying setup is correct before we layer anything on top: bid strategy, event mapping, attribution windows, creative refresh rate, budget headroom
  • Monitoring notes, what happened after it went live and whether we kept it

The tracker changes the nature of the relationship. Without it, every QBR is a debate about whether advice was followed. With it, the record is right there, and the conversation moves to what we learned instead. It also protects the advertiser from me, since a recommendation that did not work is visible too.

3. Creative breakdowns

I break creative performance down by concept, format, and hook rather than looking at a blended creative number. What I am separating is fatigue from a bad concept. Those look identical in aggregate and have opposite fixes. Fatigue shows as frequency climbing while click through rate falls on the same audience, and the answer is new executions of a concept that works. A bad concept never performed on any audience, and the answer is a different idea entirely.

On this account the breakdown showed the flagship title's creative was not the problem. The concepts still converted. The audience had simply run out. That is what told me the fix was a second title rather than a creative refresh, and it saved the advertiser a production cycle they were about to spend.

4. One thing we are changing, with a date we revert

Every week closes with a single change, a success criteria, and a date we undo it if it does not work. Stacked changes teach you nothing.

What We Did

Diversified into a second title

Rather than pushing more budget into a saturated audience, I made the case for moving incremental spend to a second title in their portfolio with a genuinely different player base. It scaled roughly 6x over the quarter and held efficiency while it did, which is the part that matters. Scaling while efficiency collapses is not growth.

Moved to a longer ROAS window

Their optimization ran on a short return window, which systematically undervalued the users the new title was bringing in. Those players monetized later. We moved to a longer window mid quarter. On the first full cohort measured the new way, cost per install came down about 7% and D7 return on ad spend improved by roughly 2.8 percentage points, from the high 23s to the high 26s.

Wound the flagship down deliberately

Not switched off. Wound down to the spend level where it was still efficient, with the headroom moved elsewhere. Advertisers rarely resist growth. They resist being told to stop doing the thing that used to work, so this needed the creative breakdown data to land first.

Challenges
  • Selling a problem they could not see: the account was performing. I had to make saturation legible before I could propose a fix, which is what the per title breakdown was for.
  • Measurement change is a trust ask: moving to a longer ROAS window means asking an advertiser to accept worse looking numbers for a few weeks before the picture corrects. That only works if the tracker has already established that my recommendations tend to hold up.
  • Honest limits: I am the strategist and the relationship owner here, not the trader. Auction level work and bid tuning ran with our programmatic team. My contribution was the diagnosis, the plan, and getting it implemented on schedule.
Results
  • Highest spend quarter on record for the account, with the growth coming from a newly scaled title rather than from pushing a saturated one harder
  • Second title scaled roughly 6x while holding efficiency
  • Cost per install down about 7% and D7 return on ad spend up about 2.8 points on the first cohort under the new measurement window
  • A written recommendation record that made their first ever quarterly business review straightforward to build and easy to defend

The transferable part is not the specific fix. It is that the cadence, the tracker, and the creative breakdowns are what made the fix findable and the advertiser willing to act on it.
