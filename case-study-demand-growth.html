The Problem

One of my largest publishers was watching DAU slide. They'd monetized with us for years and had never bought a single user through us. Their growth had always come in organically or through other channels.

Fewer players means less inventory, and less inventory means less supply revenue on my side of the book. Both halves of the account needed the same thing, so I went and made the case for UA.

Why they hesitated: they had tried UA before, on other ad networks, and hadn't gotten much back for it. The doubt was about the channel itself as much as about us. They came back with $300 a day. Next to their supply numbers, that's a rounding error.

My role: I own both sides of this relationship. I made the case for UA, ran the onboarding, built the strategy with their team, and I still own the weekly recommendation cycle.

The Call I Made

On demand spend alone, a $300 a day advertiser doesn't get an in person meeting. On the whole relationship, they're one of the biggest partners I have. I booked the meeting anyway and ran the onboarding like the T1 account it was.

A small test that's set up badly still fails, and they'd already written the channel off once on somebody else's watch.

What I Did In the Meeting

1. Started with why it hadn't worked before

They'd been burned on UA already, so I opened with that rather than around it. We went through which networks they'd run with, how those campaigns had been built, and where the results actually fell short. Then I laid out what's different here, in the product and in the level of support they'd get from me.

2. Audited the setup in the room

Attribution and event mapping, bid strategy and target ROAS, budget headroom, geo and platform splits, blocklists. Small budgets fail on setup far more often than on strategy, and a bad setup doesn't announce itself. You just see soft numbers three weeks later and start guessing at causes. Going through it together meant we caught the problems before they'd spent a dollar.

3. Taught their team the fundamentals

They didn't have a UA function, so most of what I brought was teaching rather than advice. How tROAS relates to observed ROAS, and why delivery stalls once that gap gets too wide. Why a D0 campaign lets you adjust every three days while a D28 campaign makes you wait. When to widen geos instead of reaching for the bid.

4. Brought creative ideas instead of a creative ask

Their creative was cut for their own store listing and it didn't translate to our placements. We went through what actually works on our inventory: playables that hand you a real piece of the game instead of a two tap demo, a top scaling video paired with an interactive end card, and a refresh cadence you set in advance rather than waiting for fatigue to turn up in the numbers.

5. Built the strategy with them, not for them

We picked the launch titles, the campaign structure, and the ROAS targets together, in the room. A team that helped build a plan will keep running it after I leave, and with no UA function on their side, that mattered more than the plan itself.

The Weekly Tracker

Once they were live, everything ran through one shared sheet. They fill their half by Monday and we fill ours before the call.

Issue type + priorityClientPerformance, scale, or creative, ranked so we work the right one first
Campaign + detailsClientThe specific campaign and what they are seeing, in their own words
Best practices followedClientA true or false gate. If it is false we fix the setup before anything else
RecommendationUnitySpecific enough to action without me in the room, dated when added
Monitoring notesBothWhat happened after it went live, including when it did not work
Status + last updatedBothOpen, in progress, or resolved, so nothing dies in a meeting note

The best practices column carries most of the weight. It keeps us from arguing about bids and creative while something basic is still broken, and because the client fills it in themselves, the check sits with them instead of with me.

Without a record, every review turns into a debate over whether the advice was ever followed. With one, we skip that and talk about what we learned. It works against me too, since the recommendations that flopped are sitting right there, and being straight about those is most of why they trust the ones that land.

Then We Scaled

With the setup clean and the cadence running, scaling was mostly repetition. Every title got a campaign of its own, a target, and a line in the tracker, and whatever worked on one became the starting point for the next.

Plenty of it didn't work. One title held 41% D7 ROAS at $125 a day and dropped to the low twenties when we pushed it to $300, so we pulled it back instead of buying volume at a loss. Another we paused outright and relaunched on a longer ROAS window.

They're at $5.7K a day now across a growing list of titles.

Challenges
  • Justifying the time: nothing about $300 a day says block out a day for an in person meeting. I looked at the entire relationship and the potential rather than just the revenue and spend.
  • No UA team on their side: every recommendation had to be teachable, not just right. Slower up front, much faster after.
  • Patience at small scale: numbers on a $300 a day test are noisy, and the hard part was leaving the setup alone long enough to actually read them.
  • Where I stop: bid tuning and auction level work sit with our programmatic team. I own the relationship, the strategy, the onboarding and the weekly cycle.
Results
  • $300 a day to $5.7K a day, roughly 19x, and still climbing
  • A publisher with no UA function now running it as a standing channel across multiple titles
  • New titles come to us first now, so the growth compounds without a fresh pitch each time
  • Their DAU problem getting solved on the demand side, which protects the supply revenue underneath it
