The Problem

One of my largest publishers was watching DAU decline. They monetized with us and had for years, but they had never bought a single user from us. Their whole growth motion was organic and it had stopped working.

Declining DAU on a top tier publisher is not just their problem. Fewer players is less inventory, which is less supply revenue on my side of the house too. The fix for both sides was the same: get them acquiring users again.

Why they hesitated: they had never run UA with us, they had no in house UA muscle, and they were not going to bet a real budget on a channel they could not yet evaluate. They committed $300 a day. On a spend sheet that is a rounding error.

My role: I own the relationship on both sides. I made the case for UA, ran the onboarding, built the strategy with their team, and own the weekly recommendation cycle.

The Call I Made

By demand side spend, a $300 a day advertiser does not earn an in person visit. By the full relationship, they are a top tier partner. I flew out and treated the onboarding like the T1 account it actually was.

That call is the whole case study. A $300 a day test that is set up badly fails, and a client who watches UA fail once does not come back to it for a year. The test was small. The cost of it failing was not.

What I Did On Site

1. Audited the setup in the room

Attribution and event mapping, bid strategy and target ROAS, budget headroom, geo and platform split, blocklists. Small budgets fail on setup far more often than on strategy, and setup problems are invisible from a dashboard weeks later. Sitting with their team meant we caught them before a dollar was spent.

2. Walked their team through best practices

They had no UA team, so the deliverable was not a recommendation, it was training. How tROAS relates to observed ROAS and why too wide a gap stalls delivery. Why a D0 campaign lets you adjust every three days and a D28 campaign does not. When to widen geos instead of raising bids.

3. Brought creative ideas, not a creative ask

Their creative was built for their own store listing, not for our placements. We went through what performs on our inventory: playables with a real game experience rather than a two tap demo, pairing a top scaling video with an interactive end card, and refreshing on a cadence instead of waiting for fatigue to show up in the numbers.

4. Built the strategy with them, not for them

We picked the launch titles, the campaign structure, and the ROAS targets together. A strategy their team helped build is one their team will actually run when I am not in the room, which for an account with no UA function is the difference between a channel and a one off test.

The Weekly Tracker

Once they were live, everything ran through one shared sheet. They fill their half every week by Monday. My team fills ours before the call.

ColumnOwnerWhat it does
Issue type + priorityClientPerformance, scale, or creative, ranked so we work the right one first
Campaign + detailsClientThe specific campaign and what they are seeing, in their own words
Best practices followedClientA true or false gate. If it is false we fix the setup before anything else
RecommendationUnitySpecific enough to action without me in the room, dated when added
Monitoring notesBothWhat happened after it went live, including when it did not work
Status + last updatedBothOpen, in progress, or resolved, so nothing dies in a meeting note

The best practices column does the most work. It stops the conversation from jumping to bids and creative while something basic is still broken, and it puts the check on the client rather than on me, which is what makes it stick.

The rest is a record. Without it, every review is a debate about whether advice was followed. With it, we talk about what we learned. It cuts both ways: recommendations that failed are in there too, and saying so out loud is most of why they trust the ones that work.

Then We Scaled

Once the setup was clean and the cadence was running, growth was a matter of repeating it. Each title got its own campaign, its own ROAS target, and its own line in the tracker. What worked on one became the starting point for the next.

Not everything scaled. One title held 41% D7 ROAS at $125 a day and fell to the low twenties at $300, so we pulled it back rather than buying volume at a loss. Another we paused outright and relaunched on a longer ROAS window. Those are in the tracker too.

They are at $5.7K a day now across a growing set of titles, and still adding. The channel they were not sure they wanted is now the one they bring new titles to first.

Challenges
  • Justifying the trip: nothing about $300 a day says fly out. I made the case on the full relationship, not the demand line, and it is the reason the account exists today.
  • No UA team on their side: every recommendation had to be teachable, not just correct. That is slower at the start and much faster after.
  • Patience at small scale: early numbers on a $300 a day test are noisy. The discipline was leaving the setup alone long enough to read it.
  • Where I stop: bid tuning and auction level work run with our programmatic team. Mine is the relationship, the strategy, the onboarding, and the weekly recommendation cycle.
Results
  • $300 a day to $5.7K a day, roughly 19x, and still scaling
  • A publisher with no UA function now running UA as a standing channel across multiple titles
  • New titles come to us first, so growth compounds without a new pitch each time
  • DAU pressure addressed on the demand side, which protects the supply relationship underneath it
